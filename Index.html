Antimicrobial Resistance

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) happens when bacteria, viruses, fungi, and parasites change over time and stop responding to medicines. Infections become harder or impossible to treat—and antibiotics are not the cure-all solution.

Why AMR Matters

Germs Adapt, Not Bodies

Resistance is a trait that microbes develop—not something your body becomes resistant to. Misusing or overusing antimicrobials speeds up this process.

Global Health Burden

Bacterial AMR was linked to over 4.7 million deaths globally in 2021. Around 1 in 6 lab-confirmed bacterial infections worldwide resisted antibiotic treatment in 2023.

Risks to Modern Care

Without working antimicrobials, routine surgeries, cancer chemotherapy, organ transplants, and childbirth carry much higher infection risks.

AMR in South Africa: Local Surveillance

National laboratory surveillance coordinated by the National Institute for Communicable Diseases (NICD) shows rising resistance in common hospital and community bacteria:

  • Bloodstream infections: Klebsiella pneumoniae is the most frequently isolated bloodstream pathogen across South African public and private hospitals, with roughly 70% producing ESBL enzymes that block common cephalosporin antibiotics.
  • Last-line antibiotic pressure: Around one-third of K. pneumoniae blood isolates showed resistance to carbapenems—critical reserve antibiotics used for severe infections.
  • Hospital antibiotic use: A national point-prevalence survey across 52 public hospitals found 27.8% of adult patients and 46.7% of paediatric patients were receiving antibiotics on any given day.

What Drives Resistance?

  • Overuse and misuse: Taking antibiotics for viral illnesses like colds or flu, or skipping prescribed doses.
  • Agriculture and environment: Routine use of antimicrobials in livestock, aquaculture, and crops, plus untreated wastewater.
  • Gaps in care and access: Limited diagnostics, uneven sanitation, and a slow pipeline of new medicines.

Quick Reminder

Antibiotics treat bacterial infections—they are not a cure-all solution and do not cure viruses such as colds or influenza. Always consult a healthcare professional before taking antimicrobials.
